# The intake, pass by pass

Read this at the step that asks them questions. `SKILL.md` says when a goal counts as defined, and what to do when it does not. This says what each pass digs for, with questions to start from.

A shallow intake produces a path anyone could have written from the goal's name. Run the passes in order, say what each is for, and skip what is already answered. Offer choices where the answers are predictable, and leave room for their own words.

## 1. The goal and its use

- What do you want to be able to do? Take their words verbatim; they go in the plan.
- What will you use it for: a job, an exam, a performance, a trip, your own satisfaction?
- How will you know you have got there? Who or what will judge it?
- Why now? What happens if it takes twice as long?

The use decides the standard. Conversation for a holiday and a certificate for a job are different goals with the same name. Specific goals usually beat vague ones. In Locke and Latham's review, specific, difficult goals consistently led to higher performance than urging people to do their best (2002, doi:10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705). For complex tasks, the same paper recommends specific learning goals.

When the use is their own satisfaction, nobody outside sets the standard, so they set it. Write it as something another person could check, such as a recording, a time or a finished piece. In the plan it reads `Standard: self-set:` followed by that check.

## 2. The standard and the deadline

- Is there an exam, licence, certificate, audition, competition, job or event behind this? Which one, which level, which date?
- If they do not know which standard applies, finding it is the research's first job. It is never a reason to guess one for them.

## 3. The starting point, as evidence

- What have you done in this field, or next to it? Results, scores, grades, times, recordings, work samples, with dates.
- What did you try before, and where did it stop?
- What do you already know from another field that carries over? The path teaches the difference, not the whole subject.

Ask for things someone else could look at, not a self-rating. Across 22 meta-analyses, self-evaluations of ability correlated with measured performance at .29 on average, which the authors call moderate (Zell & Krizan, 2014, doi:10.1177/1745691613518075). A result more than a year old shows where they were, not where they are.

Once the standard is known, offer a diagnostic. That is a short piece of the real assessment under its real conditions, such as an official sample paper or a timed trial. Before then there is nothing to build one from.

## 4. Constraints, as numbers

- Hours a week they can count on, net of holidays, and on which days.
- The deadline, as a date, or none.
- Money for courses, exams, equipment and fees.
- Access to equipment, a space, a teacher, practice partners, and someone who can give feedback.
- Country or jurisdiction, where licences, exams or qualifications depend on it.
- Language of instruction and of assessment.
- Physical or health limits, where the activity has them.

## 5. How they have learned before

- What kept you going last time, and what stopped you: structure, deadlines, company, cost?
- Do you want to be taught, or tested and corrected?
- Is AI help allowed while you practise? Will it be allowed where you are judged?

Do not ask for a learning style. Pashler and colleagues found virtually no evidence that matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning (2008, doi:10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x). A 2024 meta-analysis found a small matching effect, which its authors judged "too small and too infrequent to warrant widespread adoption" (Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732).

## 6. How progress is checked, and the plan delivered

- Who will check the work: you, an agent, a teacher, a coach, or only the real assessment?
- How often do you want a check?
- In what form do you want the plan, and who else will read it?

## When the goal is not defined

The passes above have been asked and the first three goal lines are still empty. Do not ask them again in the same words. Ask from a different side:

- Picture the day you would say you had arrived. What are you doing, where, and who notices?
- What would you do with it next week, if you had it now?
- What would tell you it had not worked?

Then research what their words mean in practice: the exams, certificates, standards and job requirements that sit behind them. Offer two to four concrete versions. Each names what they would be able to do, who judges it, and what the evidence says it takes. These are goals to choose between, not steps.

Where the evidence favours one version for their stated use, recommend it and say why. Where it favours none, say so. If they ask you to choose anyway, do not. The use is theirs, and a guessed goal is the guessed path this skill exists to prevent.

## Throughout

- **Their account is a hypothesis.** Test what they say about their level against pass 3. Sometimes the record says something the person does not.
- **Contradictions get written down.** Put each one back to them once, plainly. What they correct goes into the plan in their words. If there is no answer, record it under Not verified and go on with what the evidence shows.
- **Private detail stays in the conversation.** Health, money and family shape the plan through the constraint they produce, such as "4 hours a week". The reasons behind it do not go into the file.
- **Research the field, never the person.** Search the standard, the exam, the employers and the field. Never search their name.
