# Where to look, and how far to trust it

Ordered by how hard it is for a company to shape what you find. Start at the
top. Something a company had to file under legal obligation beats something it
chose to publish, which beats something written about it by someone paid to
generate traffic.

## Records a company cannot easily shape

Filings, registrations and court records exist because the law required them,
which is what makes them worth more than anything on a company website.

- **Company registries** — directors, ownership, filed accounts, charges over
  assets. UK: Companies House. US: state Secretary of State, plus SEC EDGAR
  for anything public. Czech Republic: `justice.cz` (obchodní rejstřík) and
  `ares.gov.cz`, which also carry filed financial statements. Most countries
  have an equivalent; look for it before giving up.
- **Court records** — litigation, insolvency, employment disputes. A pattern of
  the same kind of case matters more than any single one.
- **Regulators** — licences, sanctions, enforcement actions, inspection reports,
  in whichever regime governs the industry.
- **Grant and procurement databases** — public contracts and research funding
  give real revenue figures with dates attached.

Accounts are usually filed late, so treat them as a picture of the past. Small
companies often file abbreviated versions that hide revenue entirely — note
that as a gap rather than assuming the worst.

## What the company publishes

Useful for intent, weak for facts. Take the specifics and discount the framing.

- **Pricing page** — if there isn't one, the sale is probably long, negotiated
  and enterprise. That tells you about the business.
- **Customer logos and case studies** — check dates. A wall of logos with no
  case study since 2023 is a different signal from a fresh one.
- **Careers page** — what they are hiring for, and where, is the most honest
  statement of strategy a company publishes. Ten sales roles and no engineers
  means something.
- **Engineering blog, changelog, release notes** — whether the product is
  actually moving.
- **Investor and press releases** — reliable for the fact that something was
  announced, and for amounts and dates. Not reliable for what it means.

Anything phrased as a claim about the company's own performance is a claim.
Attribute it: "they say".

## Press and analysis

- Prefer the **original announcement** over coverage of it. Most articles are
  a rewritten press release, and each rewrite adds error.
- Named reporter with a beat and quotes they obtained themselves beats an
  unbylined summary.
- **Market analysis published by a company that sells into that market is
  advertising.** Check whose site you are on before citing a market-size
  number.
- **Rankings and awards** are frequently paid, self-submitted, or both. So are
  "top 10 vendors" lists. Label them.
- **Aggregator revenue estimates** — the figures on data-broker profiles are
  modelled guesses, not filings. Say so if you use one.

## Employee reviews

Real signal, badly distributed. Read them properly or not at all.

- Always give **sample size and dates**. Twelve reviews is an anecdote.
- **Read by department.** Sales and engineering often describe different
  companies, and the average conceals that.
- Look for **repeated specifics** — the same manager, the same reorganisation,
  the same broken promise about remote work. Generic praise and generic
  complaint are both noise.
- Check **local-language sites**, which are often more candid than the global
  ones and are where people actually write.
- **No reviews means no information.** It is not evidence of a good workplace.

## People

- Leadership tenure and what they did before. Short average tenure among
  executives is worth a question.
- Cross-check the **founding story** against early filings and archived
  versions of the site. If the story has changed, that is a good question, not
  necessarily a problem.
- At small companies, **advisers and investors** matter — they are the route to
  customers, and they indicate who is willing to attach their name.

## Hygiene

- **Date everything.** A fact without a date is not usable a month from now.
- **Cite where you found it**, not just that it is true.
- **Give both sides of a conflict** rather than picking the one that fits.
- **Archived pages** are legitimate sources when a company has quietly changed
  or removed something — and the change itself is often the finding.
- **Record what you could not establish.** A clearly stated gap is more useful
  than a confident guess, and it tells the reader what to ask about.
