# Career Direction

Work out where someone should be pointed, from what they have done and what the market is buying — not from what they call themselves.

The test for whether this is the right skill: they cannot name the job they want. If they can, and it exists, use `role-fit` instead.

## Stance

- **Honest, not encouraging.** A direction that flatters them wastes a year of their life.
- **A reading of evidence, not a personality test.** No types, no strengths quiz. Every line traces to something someone did, wrote or said.
- **They are not the best witness about themselves.** What four colleagues independently describe outranks what the person believes about their own work.
- **Do not forecast.** Anything about the future has to be visible in something dated today, and has to say what would prove it wrong.
- **Two answers, not one.** What pays now, and what ages well. Give only the second and they cannot act on it. Give only the first and they arrive back here in three years.
- **Test whether the change is necessary at all.** Before pricing a retraining route, check whether the thing they are reaching for already exists inside their profession, in a different industry. The pull is usually toward a kind of work rather than away from a skill, and the cheapest answer to "I want something physical and real" is often the same craft in a different building. A direction that costs nothing beats one that costs three years, and they rarely see it themselves, because they framed the question as a change.

## Keep it theirs

An intake for this skill collects more about a person than a job application does. It is worth more to a stranger than it is to them, so it goes nowhere.

- **The intake stays in the conversation.** Do not write it into files, notes, memory, a shared folder or a connected service. One file gets created, the report, and it carries conclusions rather than the raw material behind them. Say at the end what was written and where, so they can delete it if they want to.
- **Research the market, never the person.** Search for role families, skills, employers, locations and counts. Never search their name, never their name beside an employer, never a verbatim line from their history. A pasted sentence is searchable straight back to them.
- **Nothing goes into a third-party tool.** No CV scanners, no profile matchers, no site that offers to rate them. Those keep what they are given. Reading back a score a platform already shows them, on a profile they already maintain, is a different thing — nothing new is disclosed — and what it says is missing beats guessing at it.
- **The report carries a first name or initials.** No email address, no phone number, no home address, no profile link. If a CV arrives with contact details at the top, work from everything below them.
- **Ask once whether employers can be named.** A report they may forward to a recruiter is a different document from one only they will read.

## Asking

Use whatever interactive choice mechanism the environment offers — tappable options where they exist, plain questions otherwise.

Two scoping questions before any work: whether they are between jobs or employed and looking, and whether they want the whole reading or only the market half. The intake below is the work itself, not scoping, and it runs as a conversation.

**If nobody is watching** — a background job, a scheduled run, a delegated task — do not stall, and do not invent the person. Build the market half, which needs no intake, and leave the profile half out rather than guessing at it. Say at the top which half is missing and what would fill it. A confident reading of someone nobody asked is worse than half a report.

## The intake

A shallow intake produces a reading anybody could have written from a job title.

Run it in passes, in this order, and say what each pass is for. Skip what is already answered.

**1. The record.** Every role with dates, then the systems, the scale, the money, the people. Ask for the CV, read it, then ask what it leaves out. The CV is the artefact that has been failing them; treat it as evidence about their self-description rather than about their work.

**2. What other people said.** Written references, review quotes, promotion notes, what colleagues come to them for, and the rejection letters. Ask for the words, not the gist. This pass outranks everything they say about themselves, and rejection letters are the only place some employers say what was actually missing. Where a job platform already scores their profile against postings, ask them to read back the score and what it lists as missing. It is free and specific, and it measures how they are indexed rather than how they work — a different gap from the rest of this pass, and usually a cheaper one to close.

**3. What is not on the record.** Side projects, earlier careers, freelance and voluntary work, the thing they built that nobody asked for, the languages, the failure they learned from. People leave out the most relevant thing they have done because it does not match their current title. Ask directly, more than once.

**4. Energy and interest.** What they read when nobody is paying, what they would do unpaid, which part of the last job they would keep and which they would not repeat. Ask what drains them; that decides more than what they are good at, and almost nobody volunteers it.

**5. Constraints, as numbers.** Income floor as a figure rather than a feeling. Runway in months, notice period, cities and countries in and out, travel they will accept. Work rights, languages at working level, family and health. On-call, quota-carrying work, employment against contracting, and the hours a week available for the search and for any build.

**6. The clock.** Their age, what they intend to be doing at the end of the next decade, and what they are not willing to still be doing. Direction that ignores this ages badly in exactly the way they are afraid of.

Treat what they say they want as a hypothesis, and test it against passes 1 to 4. Sometimes the record says something the person does not.

**Contradictions get written down, not smoothed over.** Put each one back to them plainly, once. Whatever they correct goes in the report under corrections, in their words, including anything you had already built on.

**Stop when** the three assets can be stated in evidence that is not their own, the gap that decides can be named and corroborated twice, and the constraints are numbers. More detail past that point changes nothing but the length.

## Read the market, not the mood

**Group by what is being bought, not by job title.** The same work carries five titles across five companies, and a title search misses four of them. A family is a set of roles that buy the same asset. Name the asset.

**Count, and say where the count came from.** Demand is positions counted in a named source on a named date. An impression of a hot field is not a count, and it is the commonest way this work goes wrong.

**Count the skills, not only the seats.** Read the requirement lines across the postings and count which skills repeat. This is the concrete half of the answer: a skill named in eight adverts out of nine is a fact about this month, not a trend. Mark each one as already held or as a gap, because that is the list they can act on tomorrow.

**Search employers as well as titles.** The grouping rule above fails silently when it is only half applied: nothing is absent from a keyword search's own results, so the search looks finished. Build a list of employers who would plausibly buy the asset and read each one's own job list whole. Expect the best-fitting role of a search to be one no keyword returned, under a title nobody outside that company would think to type.

Work down the sources, hardest to fake first. Employer career pages show where the money is going. Job boards give counts, professional networks show who moved and when. Local-language boards and public-sector portals carry roles the big boards never list. Recruiter posts and communities say what the work is like.

**Read the postings, not the salary guide.** A salary guide says what a role pays. Only a posting says who they will hire, and those are different questions. Recruiter guides and market reports are advertising for a placement business: useful for the ceiling, worthless for the gate. No family goes on the list as reachable until real postings have been read and their requirement lines quoted.

**Find the feeder backgrounds.** Where they cannot enter a family directly, employers keep a list — spoken or not — of the previous jobs they accept as preparation. It is usually written down in the industry's own "how to break into this" material. Find it and check whether they are on it. Sometimes they are not, and that is the finding: the money is real, the shortage is real, and the door is shut to this person.

**Record closed and recurring postings.** A role that closed is evidence that the seat exists, not an opportunity. Mark it as such rather than counting it as live.

**Price the bar as well as the ceiling.** Every family carries two numbers and a gate: what the lowest rung pays, what the best-paying grade pays, and what that grade demands in years, tickets and licences. Say how many postings stated each figure. A family whose best-paying grade still misses their floor is out, and the report says which were cut and why, so nobody rediscovers them in a month. A family they cannot enter yet is a different thing and stays, with the gate named and the years priced, on the slow clock — cutting those is how someone loses the direction that was right but not immediate. Pricing a family at its ceiling alone is the opposite error, and how a research pass produces a confident, wrong answer.

**Stop when** three to five families are established, each with a counted demand figure, a named source, an entry bar and a pay range; at least two carry a posting read in full and at least one of those is open; and each says which asset it buys.

## The three-to-five year read

This is the half that makes the report worth keeping, and the half most likely to turn into fortune-telling. The discipline that prevents it:

- **Every shift must already be visible in something dated.** Four places a real one shows: a law with a compliance date, money moving into teams or procurement, work advertised under no settled title yet, and anything employers now pay a premium for that was free last year. Retirement curves are slower and more reliable than all four.
- **Name the mechanism, not the trend.** "This field will grow" asserts nothing. "This rule applies from that date, and somebody has to produce the evidence trail for it" can be checked, and points at who will be hiring.
- **Write what would prove it wrong.** If nothing could, cut the shift. A forecast nobody can lose is worth nothing to someone deciding what to learn.
- **Then apply the ageing test to each asset.** Does it get more valuable as they get more senior, or less? Judgement, accountability, domain knowledge and being trusted with a system age well. Raw output speed, one vendor's tooling and being the newest at something age badly. This is the axis that matters most to anyone past forty.
- **Two clocks, running together.** The fast one pays and builds the record the slow one needs. The slow one is whatever cannot be bought later — a language, a qualification, a public record, a gap that only closes with repetition. Give both, and say which gates which.
- **Put dates on the gates.** What closes, what enrols, what expires.

## The report

One self-contained page: `direction-report.html`. Fill it by replacing the data inside its `report-data` block and changing nothing else. That block is strict JSON (JavaScript Object Notation): double quotes on every key and string, no trailing commas, no comments. The page says so on screen when it is wrong.

Every section is optional and hides itself when empty, so a first pass can ship with evidence, gaps and families alone.

The example inside the page is the contract. Most keys explain themselves; a few do not. `demandN` is a count of open positions and is what the chart plots, so an estimate there is a lie with a dot on it. `pay` is what the family's best-paying reachable grade pays, and `entryBar` is what its lowest rung pays and what that paying grade demands in years, tickets and licences — both, because a family priced only at its ceiling reads as reachable when it is not. `sourcing` is `confirmed` when two independent sources back the row and `unconfirmed` when one does. `weight` is what a gap costs: decides, slows, closes doors. `when` is how soon a position is worth applying for: now, build, later. `at` and `span` place a step on a twelve-column track, and the axis labels decide what a column means, so the columns need not be evenly spaced in time.

What the page is for, and what it demands:

- **No prose.** Cards, chips, tables, a timeline, two lanes and one chart. If a point needs a paragraph, the thinking behind it is not finished.
- **Every claim names its source**, and their own account is marked as their own. The difference between "four people said it" and "he says" is the whole value of the document.
- **Nothing over about forty-five words**, and the whole report under about fourteen hundred. It is read by someone deciding, not studying. The example in the page is a full one, every section filled, at just over twelve hundred.
- **Estimates say they are estimates.** Odds are estimates. Counts are not.
- **Gaps get a weight by what they cost.** A list of weaknesses with no weights cannot be acted on.
- **End with what could not be established**, under a heading that says so. Unknown is not the same as absent, and the reader has to be able to tell which one a silence is.

The failures here are silent: a broken report looks finished. Run `scripts/report_check.py` against the filled page — `${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR}/scripts/report_check.py` in Claude Code. It reads the same data block the page does, and fails on a claim with no source, a forecast with nothing dated behind it or nothing that could disprove it, demand asserted rather than counted, a family missing its entry bar, its pay range or its corroboration mark, contact details left in the file, a step placed outside the track, and any key the page does not read. That last one matters most, because a mistyped key empties a column or drops a section while the rest still renders.

One silent failure it cannot catch: evidence that is entirely self-assessment. Marking a source as their own is a legitimate answer, so no check can tell a sourced report from one built on nothing but what they said about themselves. Count those rows yourself. Where code cannot run, check the whole list by hand.

**Work it before handing it over.** Open the page the way they will open it, click a filter, toggle the theme, and look at it at phone width. Check that every section you filled is actually on screen — a missing one is a mistyped key, not an empty life.

Where no browser can open it, write the same sections as a document instead, keeping the source marking, the gap weights and the two clocks. Only the chart is lost, and it becomes one sentence: which families are hiring now, and which of those still pay at sixty.

Then make sure they have it: attach or send the file, write it to a folder they have connected, or leave it where they can reach it, and tell them the filename either way. The page holds its own data inside it, so the file is the whole record and they can hand it to someone or delete it in one move.

## Rules

- **Two independent sources, or say plainly that it is one.** A load-bearing claim carries corroboration: not the same organisation twice, not one source quoting another, not two agencies selling into the market they are describing. Where only one source exists, keep the finding and mark it unconfirmed. An unconfirmed lead is still a lead; an unconfirmed lead dressed as a fact is what sends someone to retrain for a job that will not have them.
- Evidence from other people beats self-description. Where they conflict, give both and say which is better evidenced.
- Do not explain a finding by guessing at a cause. Report what was found.
- Correct earlier errors plainly, in the report, in the place set aside for it.
- One direction with two clocks, not five options. A list of possibilities is what they walked in with.
